If you own a pool or spa in Victoria, you'll eventually come across the term "Form 23" — usually in a letter from your local council, or when you're trying to sell a property. It sounds bureaucratic, and in a narrow sense it is: it's a numbered form. But understanding what it actually certifies, who can issue it, and what you need to do with it once you have it will save you time, money and a fair amount of stress. This guide covers all of that in plain English.

## What is a Form 23?

A Form 23, more formally the Certificate of Barrier Compliance, is the document that confirms your pool or spa's safety barrier meets the requirements of the Victorian pool and spa barrier standard at the time of inspection. It isn't a certificate about your pool's water quality, your pump, your filtration system, or anything else about the pool itself — it's specifically about the physical barrier that prevents unsupervised access, primarily by young children. That barrier typically means the fence, the gates, the latches, and the surrounding "non-climbable zone" that has to stay clear of anything that could help a child climb over.

The certificate is issued by a registered building inspector who has completed the required qualifications to assess pool barriers specifically — in Victoria, this means an inspector registered with the Building and Plumbing Commission (BPC), formerly the Victorian Building Authority (VBA). Not every registered building surveyor or inspector is automatically qualified to issue a Form 23; it requires a specific registration category, and it's worth confirming an inspector's registration before booking if you're arranging one independently.

## What does the inspection actually check?

A Form 23 inspection assesses your barrier as a complete system, not just as a fence. The main things an inspector checks include:

- **Barrier height** — generally a minimum of 1200mm above the ground on the outside of the barrier, measured correctly to account for any change in ground level.
- **Gaps** — under the fence, between vertical pickets, and anywhere else a small child could squeeze through or get a foothold.
- **The non-climbable zone** — a clear zone around the outside of the barrier, free of anything (pot plants, furniture, low walls, garden features) that could be used as a step to climb over.
- **Gates** — every gate providing access to the pool area needs to self-close and self-latch reliably, from any position, without needing to be pushed shut.
- **Any doors or windows** that open directly onto the pool area, if they form part of the barrier, since these have their own specific requirements.

The inspector will physically test gates, measure heights and gaps at multiple points along the barrier, and walk the entire perimeter checking for objects or features in the non-climbable zone. It's a hands-on, physical inspection — not a desktop review of plans or old paperwork.

## What happens if your barrier passes?

If everything checks out, the inspector issues your Form 23 Certificate of Barrier Compliance on the spot. From that point, you have 30 days to lodge the certificate with your local council. This isn't optional paperwork you can leave in a drawer — council needs the certificate on file to confirm your property's pool or spa is registered and compliant. Missing the lodgement window can result in follow-up correspondence from council and, in some cases, penalties.

Once lodged, the certificate is valid for four years. After that, you'll need a fresh inspection and a new Form 23 to stay compliant — this is the four-year re-inspection cycle that applies to every registered pool and spa in Victoria, regardless of how old the pool is or how long the barrier has been in place without issue.

## What happens if your barrier doesn't pass?

Around one in three barriers fail their first inspection, so if yours doesn't pass, you're in very ordinary company. The inspector will provide a clear list of what needs to be fixed — usually specific, actionable items like "gate hinge needs adjustment to self-close from 90 degrees" or "pot plants within 900mm of the fence need to be relocated" rather than vague generalities. Once those items are addressed, a re-inspection confirms the fix and the Form 23 is then issued. ## Who needs a Form 23, and when?

A few common situations trigger the need for a Form 23:

1. **First-time registration.** If you've never registered your pool or spa with council, you'll need an initial inspection and Form 23 as part of getting on the register.
2. **The four-year renewal.** Every registered pool needs re-certification every four years, regardless of whether anything has changed.
3. **Selling a property with a pool or spa.** Victorian vendors are required to provide a valid Certificate of Barrier Compliance to the purchaser, generally arranged before the contract of sale is signed.
4. **After a landscaping change or renovation.** If work near the pool has altered the barrier, ground levels, or non-climbable zone, it's worth getting this checked even outside the standard four-year cycle.

## Frequently asked questions about Form 23

**Does the Form 23 cover spas as well as pools?** Yes. If your spa holds more than 300mm of water, whether it's a fixed in-ground spa or a portable above-ground unit, it's treated the same as a swimming pool under Victorian barrier requirements and needs its own Form 23.

**Can I lodge the Form 23 with council myself?** Yes, lodgement with your local council is the pool owner's responsibility, though we send reminders ahead of the 30-day deadline so you don't need to track it manually.

**Is a Form 23 the same as pool registration?** Not quite. Registration is the record of your pool or spa existing on council's register; the Form 23 is the certificate confirming the barrier is compliant, which is required both for initial registration and for ongoing four-year renewals.

**What if I sell my house before the four years is up?** The existing Form 23 can generally be used for the sale if it's still within its four-year validity period and accurately reflects the barrier as it currently stands. If anything about the barrier has changed since it was issued, a new inspection is the safer option.
