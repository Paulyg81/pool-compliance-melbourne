"Pool fence requirements" is one of the more searched phrases among Victorian pool owners, and it's also one of the more misunderstood. Most owners picture a single rule — "the fence needs to be a certain height" — when in reality a compliant barrier is a system of several requirements that all need to be satisfied together. This guide breaks down each element in plain English.

## The barrier is a system, not just a fence

It helps to stop thinking of "the pool fence" as one object and start thinking of "the barrier" as everything that controls access to the pool area: the fence itself, every gate, the ground beneath the fence, and the clear zone around the outside of it. A fence that's the correct height with a faulty gate is just as non-compliant as a low fence with a perfect gate — every element has to pass on its own.

## Height

The core requirement is a minimum barrier height of 1200mm, measured on the outside of the barrier from the ground level at that point. Two details trip owners up here:

- **"Ground level" means current ground level**, not the level the fence was installed at. Landscaping, paving, added soil, or mulch build-up over the years can all reduce a barrier's effective height even if the fence itself hasn't moved.
- **Height needs to be consistent along the entire barrier**, not just at one point. A fence that's 1200mm at the front and drops to 1100mm where it meets a side boundary is non-compliant at that section, regardless of how the rest of the barrier measures up.

## Gaps

Gaps under the fence and between vertical elements (like pickets) are assessed against a maximum allowable size — generally, anything a small child could fit through or use to gain a foothold is a problem. This applies to:

- **Gaps under the fence**, which can open up over time due to ground settling or erosion.
- **Gaps between pickets**, which matters most for older timber fencing where boards can warp or shrink.
- **Gaps at boundary junctions**, where two different fence sections or materials meet and don't quite align.

## The non-climbable zone

This is the requirement most owners have never heard of until an inspector explains it, and it's one of the most common reasons a barrier fails. The non-climbable zone is a clear area required around the outside of the barrier — generally around 900mm — that needs to stay free of anything that could function as a step: pot plants, outdoor furniture, garden ornaments, wood piles, air conditioning units, low retaining walls, or dense garden growth. The logic is simple: a 1200mm fence means nothing if there's a chair sitting right next to it giving a child a boost over the top.

## Gates

Every gate providing access to the pool area needs to do two things reliably, from any open position: **self-close** and **self-latch**. This is tested by physically opening the gate to varying degrees and checking it swings shut and locks on its own every time — not just from a fully open position, and not with a push. Common gate issues include:

- Hinges that have worn or loosened over time, changing the swing behaviour.
- Self-closing springs that have lost tension.
- Latches that engage inconsistently depending on the exact angle the gate closes from.

## Boundary and house walls as part of the barrier

Where a house wall, garage wall, or existing boundary fence forms part of the pool barrier, it needs to meet the same standard as a purpose-built pool fence — including height and the absence of climbable features. Windows, low sills, or decorative elements on a wall used as part of the barrier can all present the same non-compliance risk as a gap in a standard fence.

## Above-ground pools and spas

For above-ground pools and spas, the pool or spa wall itself can serve as part of the barrier, provided it meets the height requirement and doesn't have a fixed external ladder or step giving constant access. A removable or lockable ladder preserves the wall's function as a barrier; a permanently attached one generally defeats it.

## Combining different barrier types on one property

It's increasingly common for a single pool barrier to combine several different elements — a purpose-built fence along one boundary, a section of house wall along another, and a gate connecting them. Each transition point between different barrier types is worth extra attention, since inconsistency at a junction is one of the more common ways an otherwise solid barrier fails. A frameless glass panel meeting a rendered masonry wall, for example, needs the join itself to maintain the required height and avoid creating a gap or foothold, not just each material considered separately. The same logic applies to retaining walls, boundary fences shared with a neighbouring property, and decking that changes level partway along the barrier's run.

## Gate hardware in more detail

Because gate self-closing and self-latching failure is the single most common reason a barrier doesn't pass, it's worth understanding what "reliably" actually means in an assessment. An inspector will typically open a gate to several different angles — not just fully open — and check it swings closed and engages the latch each time, without assistance. A gate that closes fine from 90 degrees but stalls or fails to latch from 30 degrees doesn't meet the standard, even though it would look and function perfectly well for everyday use. Spring tension, hinge alignment, and latch height all affect this, and any one of them drifting slightly out of adjustment over time is enough to cause a fail on an otherwise well-maintained gate.

## What this means in practice

Because the barrier is assessed as a complete system, a property can have an excellent, well-built fence and still fail an inspection over something that seems minor — a gate that doesn't quite self-latch, or a pot plant positioned a little too close to the fence line. This is normal, not a sign of a poorly built barrier, and it's why roughly one in three barriers fail their first inspection. The fixes are usually straightforward once identified.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is there a different height requirement for pools built before a certain year?** Barrier requirements apply to the current standard regardless of when the pool was installed. An older barrier that met the rules at the time it was built still needs to meet today's requirements to pass a current inspection.

**Does a house wall really need to meet the same standard as a fence?** Yes, if it's being used as part of the barrier. This is a common surprise for owners with a pool built close to the house.

**How strict is the non-climbable zone in practice?** It's assessed literally — anything that could realistically function as a step or handhold within the required clearance is a problem, regardless of how incidental its placement seems.

**What's the easiest way to check my own barrier before booking an inspection?** Walk the full perimeter and physically test each gate from a few different angles, and remove anything within about a metre of the fence that a child could stand on. It won't catch every issue, like a height measurement slightly under the requirement, but it addresses the most common causes of a first-time fail.
