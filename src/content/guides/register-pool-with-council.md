Every swimming pool and spa in Victoria — regardless of size, age, or whether it's permanent or portable — needs to be registered with the local council in the area where the property is located. If you're not sure whether your pool is registered, or you've just installed a new one, this guide covers what registration actually involves, who it applies to, and what happens after you're on the register.

## Who needs to register

The registration requirement applies broadly and isn't limited to large in-ground backyard pools. It covers:

- **In-ground and above-ground swimming pools** of any size.
- **Spas**, whether fixed or portable, provided they hold more than 300mm of water.
- **Pools on rental properties**, where the registration obligation sits with the property owner rather than the tenant.
- **Older, pre-existing pools**, which need to be registered even if they've been in place for decades and were installed before registration requirements existed.

There's a common misconception that small portable spas or older pools are somehow exempt. In practice, the water-depth threshold is what matters, not the pool's size, age, permanence, or how long it's been sitting in the backyard unregistered.

## Where to register

Registration is handled by your local council, not by a state-wide body — so the exact process, and the register your pool ends up on, depends on where the property is located. Melbourne councils each maintain their own swimming pool and spa register: for example, Bayside City Council maintains the Bayside Register of Swimming Pools and Spas, Glen Eira City Council maintains the Glen Eira Pool and Spa Register, and so on for each municipality. If you're unsure which council area your property falls under, your rates notice or a quick search of your council's website will confirm it.

## What you need to register

Most councils require the following basic information as part of registration:

- **Property details** — the address and relevant title information.
- **Pool or spa details** — type (in-ground, above-ground, spa), approximate size, and installation date if known.
- **Owner contact details.**

Registration itself is typically straightforward and can usually be completed online or via a simple form through your council. It's generally free or low-cost. What it doesn't do, importantly, is confirm that your barrier is compliant — registration and certification are two separate steps.

## Registration versus certification: what's the difference?

This distinction trips a lot of owners up. Registering your pool tells council that a pool or spa exists on your property. It's essentially a record-keeping step. Certification — getting your Form 23 Certificate of Barrier Compliance — is the separate step that confirms your barrier actually meets the required safety standard.

You need both. A registered pool without a current Form 23 is still non-compliant, and in most council processes, you can't complete registration at all without also arranging the initial compliance inspection. If you've registered your pool but never had a barrier inspection, or your Form 23 has since expired under the four-year cycle, you're not fully compliant even though your pool appears on the register.

## What happens after you register

Once registered, your property enters your council's ongoing compliance monitoring. This typically means:

- Your pool or spa is on record, and council can follow up if your certification lapses.
- You'll receive reminders (from council, and separately from us if you've booked through Pool Compliance Melbourne) as your four-year re-inspection date approaches.
- If you sell the property, the registration and certification history usually needs to be provided to the purchaser as part of the sale.

## What if you've never registered an existing pool?

This is a more common situation than owners often expect, particularly for older properties where a pool has existed for many years, sometimes changing hands between owners, without ever being formally registered. Several Melbourne councils, including Wyndham City Council, have become more active in recent years in identifying unregistered pools — including reviewing aerial imagery of established suburbs to spot pools that don't appear on the register.

If this applies to your property, the practical path forward is straightforward: register the pool with your council (this step alone doesn't carry a penalty in most cases, provided you act once you're aware of the requirement), then book a barrier inspection to get your Form 23 and complete the compliance picture. Delaying this once you know registration is required is the riskier option, since non-compliance penalties can exceed $1,600 and councils increasingly follow up proactively.

## Registering a newly installed pool

If you're having a pool or spa installed, registration should happen as soon as it's holding water — not once landscaping and final touches are complete. It's common for builders and installers to leave registration to the homeowner, so don't assume it's been handled as part of the installation contract unless it's specifically confirmed. Once the pool is registered and the barrier is finished, book your initial inspection to get your first Form 23 and start the four-year cycle from a known date.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is there a cost to register a pool with council?** Registration fees vary by council but are generally modest. Some councils don't charge a separate registration fee at all, folding it into the broader compliance process.

**Can I register a pool that was installed decades ago?** Yes — there's no time limit on registering an older pool, and doing so is the correct first step if you discover your long-standing pool was never formally registered.

**Does registration expire, like the Form 23 does?** No. Registration itself doesn't have a four-year expiry — it's your Form 23 certification that needs periodic renewal, while the underlying registration record remains current as long as the pool exists and details are accurate.

**What if I'm renting and the landlord hasn't registered the pool?** Registration and compliance obligations sit with the property owner, not the tenant. If you're renting a property with an unregistered or non-compliant pool, this is worth raising with your property manager or landlord directly.
