If there's one single component responsible for more failed pool inspections than anything else, it's the gate hinge. Not the fence, not the panels, not the non-climbable zone — the hinge and the self-closing mechanism attached to it. Owners are routinely surprised by this, because the gate looks fine, swings fine, and closes fine — right up until an inspector opens it to a specific angle and it doesn't quite make it all the way shut. This guide breaks down exactly what "self-closing" actually means under the standard, why gates that seem to work often don't meet it, and what the fix usually involves.

## What "self-closing" actually requires

The standard isn't just "the gate closes." It requires the gate to close and latch **from any open position**, without being pushed, and do so reliably every time. That's a meaningfully higher bar than most owners assume. A gate that swings shut nicely when released from fully open (90 degrees) can still fail if it doesn't have enough momentum or spring tension to complete the swing and engage the latch when released from a smaller angle, like 20 or 30 degrees. Inspectors test from multiple angles for exactly this reason — a pass from one position doesn't mean a pass from all of them.

## The hinge types you'll typically find

**Spring hinges** are the most common self-closing mechanism on standard pool gates. A coiled spring inside the hinge barrel provides the closing force, and the tension is usually adjustable — most have a small adjustment screw or removable pin that lets you increase or decrease how hard the spring pulls the gate shut. Over time, and especially with cheaper hinges, the spring can weaken, meaning the gate no longer has enough closing force to complete the swing from a partially open position.

**Hydraulic or gas-strut closers**, more common on heavier gates (glass and aluminium in particular), use a fluid-filled cylinder to control both the speed and completeness of the closing action. These are generally more reliable long-term than basic spring hinges but can fail if the internal seal degrades, leading to a gate that closes slowly, incompletely, or not at all.

**Weighted or gravity hinges**, sometimes seen on older installations, rely on the gate's own weight and a sloped hinge mechanism to swing shut under gravity. These are the most prone to failing the "any angle" test, since the closing force is weakest exactly when the gate is closest to fully open — the opposite of what's needed for a reliable self-close from any position.

## Why a gate that "looks fine" often isn't

This is the single biggest source of confusion for owners. A gate can close every single time you personally use it, simply because you're in the habit of pushing it most of the way shut without realising — muscle memory does the rest. An inspector deliberately removes that human assistance from the test, releasing the gate from a stopped position and watching whether it completes the close and the latch engages entirely on its own. If you've never actually let go of your gate partway open and watched what happens, it's worth doing before you book an inspection.

## Latch height and engagement

Beyond the swing itself, the latch has its own requirements. It needs to be positioned at a height that isn't easily reachable by a young child from either side of the gate, and it needs to engage positively — meaning it clicks fully into place, not just rests against the strike plate. A latch that "catches" some of the time but not always is a common fail, often caused by the strike plate being slightly misaligned after months of the gate settling on its hinges.

## Common reasons hinges stop performing

- **Spring tension has loosened over time** — the most frequent cause, especially on gates installed several years ago that have never had the tension re-checked.
- **The gate itself has dropped slightly on its hinges** — timber and some metal gates can sag a few millimetres over years, changing the angle at which the latch needs to engage and throwing off alignment that was fine at installation.
- **Corrosion or grime inside the hinge barrel**, particularly in coastal areas, restricting the spring's movement.
- **A DIY tension adjustment done too aggressively or not enough** — it's common for an owner to try adjusting a spring hinge themselves after a failed inspection and either barely change anything or overtighten it to the point the gate slams rather than closes cleanly, which isn't quite the same thing as reliable self-closing either.

## How this usually gets fixed

In the large majority of cases, this is a genuinely minor fix — adjusting the spring tension screw, replacing a worn spring hinge outright (inexpensive and takes minutes), or realigning a strike plate so the latch engages consistently. It's rarely a sign the whole gate needs replacing. A fencing contractor or competent handyperson can typically resolve a hinge issue in a single visit, which is exactly the kind of fix that's meant to be sorted before your included re-inspection, not a reason to assume your barrier has a deeper problem.

## What to check yourself before booking

- Open the gate to a few different positions — not just fully open — and let go each time without touching it again. Does it close and latch every time, from every position?
- Listen for the latch: it should click positively into place, not just tap against the frame.
- Check for any give or drop in the gate itself when you lift the open edge slightly — noticeable movement suggests the hinges may need attention regardless of how the closing action currently performs.

None of this replaces a proper inspection, but it's a quick way to catch the most common issue before an inspector does.

## Testing methodology inspectors actually use

It's worth understanding exactly how an inspector tests a gate, since it explains why a gate that seems fine to you can still fail on the day. Rather than opening the gate once and watching it swing shut, a proper assessment involves releasing it from several distinct positions — close to fully open, roughly halfway, and only slightly ajar — and confirming the latch engages fully each time, without any assistance. Some inspectors will also test the gate with light resistance applied, simulating a slightly sticky hinge or a gate that's picked up a little debris in its swing path, since a "perfect conditions" pass doesn't tell you much about how the gate performs on an ordinary day with wind, dust, or a slightly uneven paving stone underneath it.

## Why cheap hinges cause more repeat failures

Not all self-closing hinges are built to the same standard, and it shows up over time. Budget spring hinges, often the type included as standard with a basic flat-pack gate kit, tend to lose spring tension faster than heavier-duty commercial-grade hinges rated specifically for pool gate use. This doesn't mean a budget hinge can't pass an inspection when new — it usually does — but it's a common reason owners find themselves booking a second inspection a couple of years into their four-year cycle after a routine check reveals the tension has dropped off. If you're replacing a hinge anyway, it's worth asking your fencing contractor specifically for a pool-rated hinge rather than a general-purpose one, since the extra cost is minor and the extended reliability is meaningful over a four-year certification period.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I adjust hinge tension myself?** On most spring hinges, yes — there's usually a small adjustment point, and increasing tension slightly is a common first fix. If you're not confident, a fencing contractor can do it quickly and correctly.

**Does the latch height matter as much as the closing action?** Yes — both are assessed independently. A gate that closes perfectly but has a low, easily reachable latch is still non-compliant.

**Is a hydraulic closer better than a spring hinge?** Generally more consistent over time, but either can meet the standard if properly maintained — the mechanism type matters less than whether it's currently performing correctly.

**If my gate fails on hinges, does the rest of my barrier need re-checking too?** Your re-inspection covers the full barrier again, not just the item that failed, but a hinge-only issue on an otherwise compliant barrier is usually a fast pass once fixed.
