Renovations and landscaping projects are one of the most common ways a previously compliant pool barrier ends up non-compliant — not because anyone touched the fence itself, but because everything around it changed. New paving, a retaining wall, a deck extension, or even temporarily removing a fence section to get equipment into the backyard can all affect barrier compliance, sometimes without the owner realising until the next inspection flags it. This guide covers what to actually manage while work is underway.

## The barrier obligation doesn't pause during renovations

It's a common but incorrect assumption that compliance requirements are somehow relaxed while a property is mid-renovation. They're not. If your pool is registered and has a current Form 23, that barrier needs to remain compliant throughout any building or landscaping work happening nearby — the standard doesn't distinguish between a barrier that's non-compliant because of neglect and one that's non-compliant because a landscaper temporarily removed a fence panel to get a bobcat through.

## Common ways renovations compromise an existing barrier

**Temporary fence removal.** Contractors sometimes need to remove a section of pool fencing to move equipment or materials through a backyard. If this happens, the pool needs to be made inaccessible by some other compliant means for the duration — not simply left open with the intention of putting the fence back at the end of the day.

**Ground level changes.** Landscaping work that raises garden beds, adds paving, or builds up soil near the barrier can reduce its effective height, even if the fence itself hasn't been touched. A barrier that was a compliant 1200mm before a paving job can end up measuring less once the new surface level is accounted for.

**Materials and equipment stored near the fence.** It's common during a renovation for building materials, scaffolding, or equipment to be temporarily stacked wherever there's space — and the area just outside a pool fence is often exactly that space. This directly compromises the non-climbable zone, the same as a stack of garden pots would.

**New structures changing access.** A new deck, pergola, or outdoor room built adjacent to the pool area can introduce a new access point that wasn't part of the original certified barrier, or change how existing gates and fence lines function together.

## What to do if the fence needs to come down temporarily

If a fence section genuinely needs to be removed for the work to proceed, the pool needs an alternative compliant barrier for that period — this might mean a temporary but properly specified fencing panel system, or restricting the affected area in a way that still prevents unsupervised access to the water. It's not compliant to simply drain the pool as a substitute for a barrier unless it's genuinely empty and will remain so for the full duration the barrier is compromised, and even then, council and safety obligations around the excavation or empty shell itself may apply.

## Talking to your contractor upfront

The single most useful thing an owner can do before a renovation involving pool-adjacent work is to explicitly raise barrier compliance with the builder or landscaper before work starts, not after a section of fence has already come down. Most contractors are focused on the mechanics of the job — getting equipment in, completing the landscaping to spec — and pool barrier compliance isn't always front of mind unless the client specifically flags it as a non-negotiable constraint on how the work is sequenced.

## Getting re-inspected after the work is done

Once renovation or landscaping work near the pool is complete, it's worth booking a fresh inspection even if your existing Form 23 hasn't technically expired yet. This isn't a strict legal requirement in every case, but given how often ground level changes, new plantings, or altered access points affect compliance without being obvious to the owner, a post-renovation check is the most reliable way to confirm the barrier that looked fine before the work started still meets the standard now that it's finished.

## What this means for your renovation budget and timeline

Building barrier compliance into your renovation planning — rather than discovering an issue afterward — is the difference between a minor adjustment and a compliance gap that sits unnoticed for months. If your renovation plans include anything that touches the ground level, fence line, or access points around your pool, it's worth factoring a post-work inspection into your project timeline and budget from the outset, the same way you'd budget for final council sign-off on other aspects of the build.

## Coordinating trades when several are working near the pool

Larger renovations often involve multiple trades moving through the same backyard space over weeks — landscapers, concreters, a pool resurfacing contractor, an electrician running new lighting. Each one individually might have a legitimate, brief reason to access the pool area in a way that touches the barrier, and it's easy for compliance to fall through the gaps between them if no single person is treating it as their responsibility. It's worth appointing yourself, or explicitly asking your builder to take on, the role of confirming the barrier is restored to a compliant state at the end of each day any trade has been working in that zone, rather than assuming whoever was last on site handled it.

## Insurance considerations worth knowing about

Beyond the compliance obligation itself, it's worth being aware that some home and landlord insurance policies have conditions tied to pool barrier compliance, and a period of non-compliance during renovation works — however temporary — is technically a lapse from the policy's perspective if an incident were to occur during that window. This is a reasonable additional argument, beyond the legal requirement itself, for treating temporary barrier compromises during a renovation as seriously as a permanent one.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to notify anyone before temporarily removing pool fencing for renovation access?** There's no separate notification requirement specific to this, but the pool needs to remain inaccessible by some compliant means for the entire period the fence is down — this is your responsibility as the owner to manage, regardless of who's doing the physical work.

**Will my existing Form 23 automatically become invalid once renovation work starts?** Not automatically, but if the work changes the barrier, ground level, or access points it's based on, the certificate may no longer accurately reflect your actual barrier — which is why a post-renovation check is worth booking regardless of the certificate's remaining validity.

**Does this apply to renovations that don't touch the pool area at all?** If the work is genuinely unrelated and doesn't affect ground levels, access, or materials storage anywhere near the barrier, there's no specific additional obligation — the concern is specifically about work that intersects with the barrier zone in some way.
