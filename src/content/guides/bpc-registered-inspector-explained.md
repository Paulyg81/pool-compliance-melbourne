If you've looked into booking a pool inspection in Victoria, you've probably seen the term "BPC-registered" attached to almost every legitimate provider. It's not just a marketing phrase — it refers to a specific, checkable qualification that determines whether the Form 23 you're issued actually means anything. This guide explains what it is, why it matters, and how to confirm it before you book.

## What the BPC is

The Building and Plumbing Commission (BPC) is the Victorian body responsible for regulating building practitioners, including the inspectors qualified to assess pool and spa barrier compliance. It was formerly known as the Victorian Building Authority (VBA); the function is the same, just under a different name following a machinery-of-government change. If you come across older references to VBA registration, they're describing the same underlying system.

## Why registration matters for a Form 23

A Form 23 Certificate of Barrier Compliance is a legal document. It can only be validly issued by a building surveyor or inspector who holds the correct registration category with the BPC, specifically covering pool and spa barrier compliance. Not every building professional holds this — a general building inspector, a real estate building and pest inspector, or an unlicensed handyman offering to "check your fence" cannot issue a valid Form 23, regardless of how thorough their assessment looks.

This matters practically in two situations:

1. **If you need the certificate for council registration or the four-year renewal**, an invalid certificate simply won't be accepted, and you'll need to start again with a properly registered inspector.
2. **If you need the certificate for a property sale**, providing one that wasn't issued by a correctly registered inspector doesn't satisfy the vendor's legal obligation, and it can surface as a problem during conveyancing.

## What registration actually covers

BPC registration in the relevant category confirms an inspector has met the qualification, training, and ongoing professional requirements to assess buildings — including pool barriers — against the applicable standards. It's a check on competency and accountability: registered inspectors operate under a professional framework with obligations around how assessments are conducted and recorded, rather than working entirely on their own informal judgement.

## How to confirm an inspector's registration

If you're arranging an inspection independently rather than through a booking service, it's reasonable to ask directly:

- Confirm the inspector's name and registration number before the visit.
- Ask which specific registration category they hold, since BPC registration covers a range of building practitioner types, not just pool barrier assessment.
- A legitimate Form 23, once issued, should identify the inspector clearly — this is worth checking on the certificate itself, not just taking on trust.

## Why this is easy to overlook

Most pool owners have no reason to know the difference between a general building inspection and a specifically BPC-registered barrier assessment, and unfortunately that gap is sometimes exploited by providers offering a cheaper, faster "inspection" that isn't actually valid. It's rarely deliberate deception — more often it's an inspector genuinely believing their general qualifications extend further than they do, or a booking service that hasn't carefully vetted who they dispatch. Either way, the outcome is the same: a certificate that doesn't hold up when it matters.

## Finding out your existing certificate wasn't properly issued

Occasionally an owner discovers, usually when trying to lodge a certificate with council or during a property sale, that a previous inspection wasn't actually carried out by someone holding the correct BPC registration. This is more stressful than it needs to be — it doesn't mean your barrier is necessarily non-compliant, only that the paperwork confirming it isn't valid. The fix is a new inspection by a properly registered inspector, which will either confirm your existing barrier is fine and issue a valid Form 23, or identify what needs adjusting. It's an inconvenience and an avoidable cost, but not a sign you've done anything wrong as the property owner — the responsibility for holding correct registration sits with the person or business offering the inspection service.

## What this doesn't mean

Being BPC-registered doesn't mean every inspector applies the standard identically, and it doesn't mean two inspectors will never reach slightly different conclusions on a genuinely borderline barrier feature. What it does mean is that the assessment is being made by someone qualified and accountable to do so, working from the same regulatory standard, rather than an informal opinion with no professional weight behind it.

## Why booking services should be transparent about this

Because most owners can't easily tell a properly registered inspector from one who isn't just by looking at a quote or a website, the burden of getting this right shouldn't sit with the customer. A booking service that dispatches inspectors on your behalf should be upfront about how it vets registration, rather than leaving you to independently verify credentials for someone you were referred to. If a provider is vague or evasive when asked how they confirm an inspector's BPC registration, that's worth treating as a warning sign rather than a minor gap in their marketing material.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is BPC registration the same as being a licensed builder?** No — it's a separate registration category specific to building surveying and inspection functions, including pool barrier compliance. A licensed builder doesn't automatically hold this.

**Can I check an inspector's registration myself?** Yes, the BPC maintains a public register of licensed and registered building practitioners that can be searched to confirm an individual's current registration status.

**Does the VBA still exist, or has it been fully replaced by the BPC?** The Victorian Building Authority's functions were transferred to the Building and Plumbing Commission. Older paperwork or certificates referencing the VBA from before this change remain valid; new certificates are issued under BPC registration.

**Why does this matter more for pool barriers than other building work?** Because a Form 23 has a direct legal consequence — council registration, sale settlement, and penalty exposure all hinge on it being valid — the qualification of whoever issues it isn't a minor technicality. It's the thing that makes the certificate actually count.

**Does registration expire, or is it a one-time qualification?** BPC registration is maintained on an ongoing basis under the Commission's professional framework, rather than being a qualification obtained once and held indefinitely regardless of conduct or currency. This is part of why working with a currently registered inspector matters, rather than relying on a qualification someone held at some point in the past.
