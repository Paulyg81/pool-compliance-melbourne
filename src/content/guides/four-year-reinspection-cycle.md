One of the most misunderstood parts of Victorian pool compliance is that a passed inspection isn't a one-off event — it's the start of a four-year clock. Every registered pool and spa barrier in the state needs to be re-inspected and re-certified every four years, indefinitely, for as long as the pool exists. This guide explains how the cycle works, why it exists, and the most practical way to stay ahead of it rather than getting caught out.

## Why a four-year cycle exists at all

It would be reasonable to assume that a compliant barrier, once built and certified, would simply stay compliant — after all, a fence doesn't typically change on its own. In practice, barriers drift out of compliance far more often than owners expect, for reasons that have nothing to do with the fence being poorly built in the first place:

- **Ground levels change.** Landscaping, new paving, added decking, or even gradual soil build-up can quietly reduce a barrier's effective height over years.
- **Hardware wears.** Gate hinges, self-closing springs and latches are mechanical components that degrade with age and use, the same as any other hardware exposed to the weather.
- **Gardens grow.** Trees and shrubs planted well clear of a fence line can, over four years, grow branches or dense foliage that overhangs or presses against the barrier.
- **Renovations happen.** Decking, retaining walls, and landscaping projects near a pool don't always get checked against barrier requirements once finished, even when the pool fence itself isn't touched.

The four-year cycle exists because these changes are gradual and easy to miss without a dedicated, physical re-inspection. It isn't a revenue mechanism or box-ticking exercise — it reflects the genuine, observed rate at which previously compliant barriers stop being compliant for entirely unremarkable reasons.

## How the cycle actually works

The clock starts from the date your Form 23 Certificate of Barrier Compliance is issued, not from when you lodge it with council (though the two dates are usually close together, since you're required to lodge within 30 days of issue). Four years from that issue date, your certificate expires, and a new inspection is required to bring it current again.

This applies uniformly — there's no exemption for a barrier that clearly hasn't changed, no discount for a newer pool, and no grace period built into the requirement itself. Every registered pool and spa in Victoria is on this same four-year schedule, whether it was installed forty years ago or four months ago.

## What happens if your certificate lapses

If your four-year certificate lapses without a new inspection, your pool or spa effectively falls out of compliance in council's records, even if the physical barrier hasn't changed at all. Councils actively monitor registration and certification status, and a lapsed certificate can trigger a compliance notice or follow-up correspondence. Non-compliance penalties for Victorian pool and spa owners can exceed $1,600, which is a considerable cost compared to the $240 flat fee for a standard re-inspection.

Beyond the direct penalty risk, a lapsed certificate can also complicate selling a property. Vendors are required to provide a valid Certificate of Barrier Compliance as part of selling a home with a pool or spa, and discovering that your certificate expired months ago — partway through a sale campaign — is a stressful and entirely avoidable situation.

## How to stay ahead of your renewal

The most reliable way to avoid a lapsed certificate is simply knowing your expiry date and acting on it before council needs to remind you. A few practical habits help:

1. **Note the exact issue date** of your current Form 23, and calendar the four-year expiry the day you receive it.
2. **Book your re-inspection a few weeks early**, rather than right on the expiry date — this gives you buffer time if the barrier doesn't pass the first time and needs a follow-up.
3. **Keep a copy of your Form 23** somewhere accessible, since you'll want to reference the issue date and may need it again if you sell the property before the next renewal is due.
4. **Flag any landscaping or renovation work** near the pool to whoever inspects it next, even if it happens partway through the four-year cycle, since changes to ground levels or nearby structures are a common source of new non-compliance.

## Does the four-year cycle apply to spas too?

Yes. Any spa holding more than 300mm of water — whether it's a fixed in-ground spa or a portable above-ground unit — is subject to exactly the same registration, certification and four-year re-inspection requirements as a swimming pool. There's no separate, lighter-touch cycle for spas.

## What if I bought a property and don't know when the certificate is due?

If you've purchased a property with an existing pool or spa, the previous Form 23's issue date should have been provided as part of the sale, since a valid certificate is a legal requirement of the sale itself. If you don't have that paperwork, your local council will hold a record of the property's registration and certification history and can confirm the current status and next renewal date.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the four-year cycle reset if I make repairs to the barrier?** No — repairs or fixes made to pass an inspection don't reset the clock beyond the standard four years from the date the resulting Form 23 is issued. A brand-new inspection following any repairs starts a fresh four-year period from that new certificate's issue date.

**What if my pool has never been inspected at all?** You'll need an initial inspection and Form 23 to get onto the register in the first place, at which point the standard four-year cycle begins.

**Is there a fine for missing the four-year deadline even by a short time?** Enforcement varies by council, but the underlying non-compliance penalty framework applies regardless of how briefly a certificate has lapsed, so it's worth avoiding rather than testing.
