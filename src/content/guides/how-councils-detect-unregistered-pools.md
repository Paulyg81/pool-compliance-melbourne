Every year, a number of Victorian pool and spa owners hear from their council about a pool that was never registered — sometimes one they've owned for years, sometimes one that came with the property when they bought it. This isn't random enforcement. Councils have become increasingly systematic about identifying unregistered pools, and understanding how the process works helps explain why it's a better idea to get ahead of it than to wait and see.

## Why councils are actively looking

Pool and spa drowning remains one of the more preventable causes of injury and death among young children in Victoria, and unregistered pools sit entirely outside the compliance system meant to prevent it — no barrier check, no Form 23, no four-year renewal reminder. From a council's perspective, an unregistered pool isn't just an administrative gap; it's a property with a swimming pool that has never been confirmed to have a compliant safety barrier. That's the underlying reason enforcement activity has increased across many municipalities in recent years.

## Aerial and satellite imagery

The most commonly used method is comparing aerial or satellite imagery against the council's existing pool and spa register. A swimming pool is a distinctive, easily identified feature from above, and modern imagery is detailed enough to spot even smaller above-ground pools and spas. Councils cross-reference what's visible in imagery against properties already on the register, and any pool-shaped feature that doesn't match an existing registration becomes a candidate for follow-up.

This approach has been particularly visible in Melbourne's growth corridor councils — areas like Wyndham, which has a large number of newer pools installed alongside recent home builds and has been active in using imagery review to identify gaps in registration.

## Building permits and construction records

Pools installed with a building permit — which is required for most in-ground and many above-ground installations — leave a paper trail that councils can cross-reference against the pool register. A permit issued for pool construction that never shows up as a subsequent registration is a straightforward mismatch to flag.

## Property sales and title transfers

Conveyancing processes increasingly surface pool compliance status, since vendors are required to provide a valid Form 23 as part of selling a property with a pool. This creates natural touchpoints where an unregistered or uncertified pool becomes visible — either because a conveyancer or purchaser's solicitor asks the question directly, or because the sale itself prompts an owner to sort out compliance they'd previously overlooked.

## Complaints and reports

Councils also receive reports from neighbours, tradespeople, real estate agents, and other members of the public who notice a pool that doesn't appear to have appropriate fencing. This is a less systematic pathway than imagery review, but it remains a real source of follow-up activity, particularly for older or visually obvious barrier issues.

## What happens once a council identifies a gap

If your council identifies your property as having an apparently unregistered pool, the typical process is a letter or notice requesting you register the pool and arrange a compliance inspection within a specified timeframe. This is generally not an immediate penalty — most councils treat the first contact as a prompt to act, not an automatic fine — but ignoring it, or continuing to delay, does increase the risk of formal enforcement action and penalties, which can exceed $1,600.

## Why it's better to register proactively

If you already suspect your pool isn't registered — perhaps you inherited it with the property, or it's been there long enough that you're genuinely unsure — there's a clear practical advantage to registering and booking an inspection before council contacts you rather than after. Acting proactively puts you in control of timing, avoids the pressure of a compliance deadline set by someone else, and generally reflects better in any follow-up correspondence than responding only after being prompted.

## Common reasons pools end up unregistered

A few patterns show up repeatedly:

- **Older pools installed before registration was mandatory**, where an owner has simply never revisited the requirement since.
- **Properties that changed hands** without registration details being carried over or confirmed by the new owner.
- **Above-ground and portable pools and spas**, where owners genuinely don't realise the same registration rules apply as to an in-ground pool.
- **Pools installed as part of a new build**, where the owner assumed registration was handled automatically as part of the building process, when in practice it's a separate step.

## What to do if you're unsure about your own property

If you're not certain whether your pool or spa is currently registered, the most reliable path is checking directly with your local council rather than guessing. Each Victorian council maintains its own register — for example, the Bayside Register of Swimming Pools and Spas, or the Wyndham Pool and Spa Register — and can confirm your property's status. If it turns out registration is missing, the next step is straightforward: register the pool, then book a barrier inspection to bring the compliance picture fully up to date.

## Does this vary a lot between councils?

Yes — enforcement intensity and methodology aren't uniform across Victoria's councils. Faster-growing municipalities with a lot of recent pool installations, particularly across Melbourne's outer growth corridors, have generally been more active in using imagery review, simply because there's a larger pool of newer, potentially unregistered installations to check against. Established inner and middle-ring councils tend to see more of their unregistered pools surface through property sales and complaints rather than large-scale imagery sweeps, given slower rates of new pool construction. Regardless of which pattern applies in your area, the underlying registration requirement is identical statewide.

## The cost of waiting versus acting now

It's worth being direct about the trade-off. Registering and booking an inspection proactively costs the same as it would if prompted by a council letter — there's no proactive discount, and there's no penalty for having gone unregistered up to that point, provided you act once you're aware. What changes is the pressure and timeline: acting on your own schedule versus responding to a compliance notice with an implied deadline. Given the cost of an inspection is a flat $240 either way, there's little practical reason to wait for the letter rather than getting ahead of it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will I be fined automatically if my council finds an unregistered pool?** Typically no — most councils issue a notice prompting registration and inspection first, with penalties reserved for continued non-compliance after that. Processes do vary by council, though.

**Does registering late count against me?** Registering as soon as you become aware of the requirement is generally viewed favourably. The bigger risk comes from ignoring a known compliance gap rather than from the pool having been unregistered for some period beforehand.

**How accurate is aerial imagery review, really?** It's accurate enough to reliably identify the presence of a pool or spa, though it can't assess barrier compliance itself — that still requires a physical, on-site inspection.

**What if the pool belonged to a previous owner and I didn't know it wasn't registered?** This is a common situation and isn't held against you as deliberate non-compliance. The practical next step is the same regardless of how the gap arose: register the pool and arrange an inspection.
