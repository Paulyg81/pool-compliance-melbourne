About one in three pool and spa barriers fail their first inspection in Victoria. That figure surprises a lot of owners — most people assume a fence that's been standing for years without issue is fine, and in the everyday sense, it probably is. But the barrier standard is specific and unforgiving about details that don't affect how a fence looks or functions day-to-day, and those details are exactly what an inspector is trained to check. This guide walks through the most common reasons barriers fail, and what's usually involved in fixing each one.

## Gates that don't self-close and self-latch properly

This is, by a wide margin, the most common reason a barrier fails. Every gate providing access to a pool or spa area needs to close and latch on its own, from any open position, without being pushed or pulled. A gate that swings shut easily from fully open but doesn't quite make it from a 45-degree angle doesn't meet the standard, even though it might look and function perfectly well in daily use.

**Why it happens:** Hinges wear, gate weight shifts slightly over time, self-closing springs lose tension, and ground settling changes the angle a gate needs to travel. None of this is usually visible without specifically testing the gate from multiple positions.

**How it's fixed:** Usually a hinge adjustment, a new or re-tensioned self-closing spring, or in some cases a latch replacement. These are typically inexpensive fixes that a handyman or fencing contractor can complete quickly.

## Objects within the non-climbable zone

The non-climbable zone is the clear area required around the outside of a pool barrier — generally 900mm — that must be kept free of anything that could give a child a foothold to climb over. This trips up a huge range of otherwise well-maintained properties, because the objects involved are often things owners consider completely unrelated to pool safety: garden furniture, pot plants, garden ornaments, wood piles, air conditioning units, and even low retaining walls or garden edging.

**Why it happens:** These items usually get placed for entirely reasonable, unrelated purposes — somewhere to put the pot plants, a spot for the outdoor furniture that's out of the way of foot traffic — without anyone specifically checking their position against the barrier's climbing zone.

**How it's fixed:** In most cases, simply relocating the object further from the fence line resolves it. Where a permanent structure like a retaining wall is involved, the fix can be more involved, sometimes requiring a barrier redesign in that section.

## Barrier height below the required minimum

Pool and spa barriers need to be a minimum of 1200mm high, measured from the ground on the outside of the barrier. This sounds like a simple measurement, but it's assessed at the actual, current ground level — not the level the fence was built at, if landscaping, paving, or turf has changed the ground height since installation.

**Why it happens:** New paving, raised garden beds, added decking, or even a build-up of mulch or soil against a fence line can quietly reduce the effective height of an otherwise compliant barrier over months or years.

**How it's fixed:** Sometimes it's as simple as removing built-up material from the base of the fence. In other cases, a fence extension or a section rebuild is required to restore the correct height.

## Gaps under or within the fence

Any gap under the fence larger than 100mm is generally non-compliant, since it can allow a small child to crawl underneath. Gaps between vertical pickets are also checked, along with any horizontal rails that could be used as a climbing rung on the outside of the fence.

**Why it happens:** Ground settling, erosion, or simply an original installation that didn't account for uneven ground can leave gaps. Horizontal-rail fencing, common in older properties, can also present a climbing aid if rails are spaced in a way that creates a usable step.

**How it's fixed:** Backfilling with compacted material, adding a check rail or ground-level infill, or in the case of climbable horizontal rails, adding a barrier extension on the outside face to remove the foothold.

## Vegetation encroaching on the barrier

Established gardens are one of the more overlooked causes of a failed inspection. Trees, shrubs and hedges planted well clear of a new fence line can, over years, grow branches that overhang the barrier or create dense foliage against it that a child could use to climb.

**Why it happens:** Plant growth is gradual and easy not to notice day-to-day, especially for owners who aren't specifically thinking about barrier compliance when they garden.

**How it's fixed:** Pruning back overhanging branches and clearing dense growth from the non-climbable zone is usually sufficient, though established trees positioned very close to the fence sometimes require more significant work.

## Above-ground pool access points

For above-ground pools, the pool wall itself can serve as part of the barrier — provided it meets the height requirement and doesn't have permanent external access. Fixed ladders, steps, or decking that provide constant access over the pool wall defeat this purpose entirely, turning a compliant wall into a non-compliant one.

**Why it happens:** Fixed steps and ladders are often installed for everyday convenience without the owner realising they compromise the barrier function of the pool wall.

**How it's fixed:** Replacing a fixed ladder with a removable or lockable one, or fitting a self-closing gate at the top of any fixed access point.

## Inconsistent sections at boundary junctions

Where a pool barrier changes material or transitions between sections — glass to masonry, a house wall to a side fence, an old section to a newly renovated one — inconsistencies in height, gaps, or non-climbable clearance are common.

**Why it happens:** Renovations, partial upgrades, and multi-material fencing designs don't always get checked as a complete system once installation is finished.

**How it's fixed:** Bringing the weaker section up to the same standard as the rest of the barrier, which can range from a minor adjustment to a partial rebuild depending on the specific issue.

## What to check before your inspector arrives

Walking your own barrier before an inspection can catch some of the more obvious issues:

- Push each gate from a few different open positions and confirm it swings shut and latches every time.
- Walk the full outside perimeter and remove anything within about a metre of the fence — pots, furniture, garden tools, anything stackable.
- Look for gaps under the fence, especially in corners and low spots.
- Check for any overhanging branches or dense foliage against the barrier.

None of this guarantees a pass — some issues, like a barrier height that's marginally under the requirement, need proper measurement to identify — but it can meaningfully improve your odds and save time if a re-inspection is needed.
