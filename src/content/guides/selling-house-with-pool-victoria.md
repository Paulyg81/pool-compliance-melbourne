Selling a home with a pool or spa in Victoria comes with an extra compliance step that catches a lot of vendors off guard, usually because it surfaces partway through a sale campaign rather than at the very start. If your property has a pool or spa, you're required to provide a valid Certificate of Barrier Compliance (Form 23) as part of the sale. This guide covers what that requirement means in practice, when to arrange it, and how to avoid it becoming a last-minute scramble.

## Why this matters for vendors specifically

Victorian law requires that a valid Form 23 be provided to a purchaser when selling a property with a pool or spa. This is distinct from a standard building or pest inspection that a buyer might arrange independently — it's a vendor obligation tied specifically to the pool or spa's barrier compliance, and it needs to be current (within its four-year validity period) at the time of sale.

If you don't have a current Form 23, or your existing one has expired, this isn't something that can simply be skipped or handled informally. It's a legal requirement, and conveyancers and buyers' solicitors increasingly check for it as standard practice during a Victorian property sale.

## When to arrange your pre-sale inspection

The most common mistake vendors make is leaving this until a buyer's solicitor or conveyancer asks for it during due diligence — often only a couple of weeks before settlement, when time pressure makes any barrier issues far more stressful to resolve. The better approach is to treat your pool barrier inspection as one of the very first things you organise once you decide to sell, alongside getting photos taken and choosing an agent.

Arranging it early gives you time to deal with the fact that around one in three barriers fail their first inspection. If yours does, you'll need to make repairs and have a re-inspection before a Form 23 can be issued — and doing this before your campaign launches, rather than during a live sale with an anxious buyer waiting, is considerably less stressful.

## What if your existing Form 23 is still valid?

If you already have a Form 23 that's within its four-year validity period and accurately reflects your current barrier, you may be able to use it for the sale without a fresh inspection — provided nothing about the barrier has changed since it was issued. If you've done any landscaping, decking, or renovation work near the pool since your last certificate, it's worth getting a fresh inspection regardless of the certificate's remaining validity, since those changes are a common source of new non-compliance that the existing paperwork won't reflect.

## What happens if your barrier fails during a sale

If your pre-sale inspection turns up issues, the inspector will provide a clear list of what needs fixing. Common examples include gates that don't self-close properly, garden features within the non-climbable zone, or barrier height that's dropped below the required minimum due to landscaping changes. Most of these are quick, inexpensive fixes — a gate hinge adjustment, moving some pot plants, trimming overgrown foliage — but they do need to be actioned and re-inspected before a compliant Form 23 can be issued.

This is exactly why arranging your inspection early matters. A fail discovered eight weeks before your campaign launches is a minor inconvenience. The same fail discovered five days before settlement is a genuine problem that can hold up the sale.

## Does the buyer's inspection cover this?

No. A standard pre-purchase building and pest inspection, which many buyers arrange independently, typically does not include a specific pool barrier compliance assessment against the Form 23 standard. It might note the general presence and condition of a pool fence, but it isn't a substitute for the vendor's Form 23 obligation. Don't assume a clean building inspection report means your pool barrier is compliant — the two assessments are different in scope and purpose.

## What if you're selling a property with an unregistered pool?

If your pool has never been registered with council — which does happen, particularly with older properties — this needs to be sorted before a valid Form 23 can be issued, since certification and registration are connected processes. It adds a step to the timeline, so if you're at all unsure whether your pool is currently registered, it's worth confirming with your local council as early as possible in your sale preparation.

## A practical pre-sale timeline

1. **As soon as you decide to sell:** book your pool barrier inspection. This gives maximum buffer time if repairs and a re-inspection are needed.
2. **If the barrier fails:** action the repairs promptly and book your included first re-inspection within 60 days to keep it free.
3. **Once you have a current Form 23:** keep it with your other sale documents (title, building reports, other disclosures) ready to provide to the buyer's conveyancer.
4. **At settlement:** the valid Form 23 should be handed over as part of the standard sale documentation.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a new Form 23 even if my current one hasn't expired?** Not necessarily, provided it's still within its four-year validity and the barrier hasn't changed since it was issued. If you're unsure, a fresh inspection removes any doubt and avoids a dispute arising during conveyancing.

**What if the buyer wants their own inspection anyway?** That's their choice to arrange, but it doesn't remove your obligation as the vendor to provide a valid Form 23.

**How long does the whole process take?** A standard inspection can typically be scheduled within 48 hours of booking, with the Form 23 issued on the day if the barrier passes. If repairs are needed, timing depends on how quickly the specific fix can be completed, though most issues we see are resolved within a few days.

**Does this apply to spas too, not just full-size pools?** Yes — any spa holding more than 300mm of water needs a valid Form 23 for the sale, exactly the same as a swimming pool.
